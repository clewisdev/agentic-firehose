## Summary

Justin argues that software engineers aren't becoming obsolete in the AI era—instead, the discipline of software engineering itself is becoming universally applicable. The core claim: engineers' natural mental models (problem decomposition, identifying primitives, systems thinking) translate directly to any domain in an AI-native world.

The post reframes "software engineer" as a skill set, not a job title. Key insight: the ability to apply engineering methodology—functional/non-functional requirements, entity modeling, interface design, iterative optimization—works whether you're building infrastructure, designing brands with Midjourney, or processing medical lab records.

He walks through two concrete examples:

**Design case**: Building brand assets using Midjourney. Started with functional/non-functional questions (what emotion does the brand evoke? what must it avoid?), identified core entities (color, type, photography, voice, motion), created a system of guidelines (interface) that an agent or designer could follow, then used clustering/scaffolding to explore the design space. Final artifact: a creative brief that could be handed off.

**Medical data case**: Consistency in processing lab records. Core entities: analytes, reference intervals, technician comments. Problem: this data manifests radically differently across lab companies. The decomposition reveals where the real work is (normalizing inputs before processing) vs. where AI agents can be effective.

The post echoes a broader industry sentiment (quoted from Aaron Levie, Xiaoyin Qu, Elizabeth Stone): agents are the single biggest form of technical leverage in history. The competitive advantage isn't "knowing how to code"—it's knowing how to think systematically across domains.

## Key Quotes

> "Software engineers can do any job now. The world (read: white collar work) is literally mostly just software."

> "The ability to decompose a problem and identify the common set of primitives that make up the whole are two core engineering concepts that translate to every domain now."

> "The goal of any engineer is not to memorize these steps but to walk away with a general-purpose thinking loop to form the right outcome. This is the exact same mindset required to building anything in AI today!"

> "This is where it's critical to find where your time is most valuable."

## Takeaways

- **Skill transfer as leverage**: Engineering methodology (decomposition, requirement elicitation, entity modeling, iterative refinement) applies across design, medical data, product, and any domain that involves constraint-satisfaction.
- **Systems thinking as differentiator**: The post echoes Netflix CPO Elizabeth Stone's claim that cross-domain visibility is now the most valuable skill. Engineers have this naturally.
- **AI changes the interface, not the thinking**: Agents become the interface for executing the design once the engineer has clarified the spec (requirements, entities, constraints). The design work remains non-delegable.
- **Timing and scope matter**: Example of design work: useful to build a creative brief, but not the final artifact. Knowing when to hand off vs. iterate is domain-specific judgment.
- **Generalist premium in 2026**: Quoting Xiaoyin Qu: "English + any other skill" dominated the late 90s. Now it's "engineering thinking + any domain."

## Open Questions

- How does this reframing hold when domain complexity is very high (e.g., medical, regulatory, safety-critical)? Is "systems thinking" sufficient or do you still need domain-specific apprenticeship?
- The post claims system architecture interviews predict "how someone thinks." Does that predictive power hold across non-software domains, or does the specificity of CS preparation matter more than he suggests?
- How do you avoid the trap of treating every domain like a software system? Where does analogical thinking break down?
